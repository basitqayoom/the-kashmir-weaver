## Before the Common Era

The high-altitude regions of the Himalayas and Tibetan Plateau had long been home to pastoral communities raising hardy mountain goats adapted to extreme climates. Over generations, these animals developed the exceptionally fine insulating undercoat that would later become known as pashmina.

Trade routes gradually connected these remote landscapes with Kashmir, Central Asia, Persia, and the Indian subcontinent, allowing valuable fibres and textiles to travel beyond their places of origin.

---

## First Millennium CE

Kashmir emerged as an important centre of learning, art, and craftsmanship. Although surviving evidence is limited, textile production flourished alongside developments in philosophy, literature, architecture, and religious scholarship.

The valley’s position along major trade routes encouraged exchanges of artistic ideas and materials.

---

## 13th–15th Centuries

Persian artistic traditions increasingly influenced Kashmir.

Floral ornament, refined colour palettes, and sophisticated decorative aesthetics became more prominent.

Many of the design principles later associated with classical Kashmiri shawls can be traced to this period of cultural interaction.

---

## The Mughal Era (16th–18th Centuries)

The arrival of the Mughals marked one of the great periods in the history of Kashmiri pashmina.

Imperial patronage encouraged exceptional craftsmanship.

Court workshops refined weaving techniques.

Demand for luxurious shawls increased across the empire.

Many of the masterpieces preserved in museum collections today reflect the artistic excellence achieved during this era.

---

## The Afghan Period (1752–1819)

Political change brought new challenges for artisans.

Despite periods of instability, weaving traditions continued.

Knowledge survived because artisan families preserved their skills across generations.

---

## The Sikh Period (1819–1846)

Shawl production remained an important economic activity.

Trade continued, although political circumstances evolved.

Traditional workshops adapted while maintaining their commitment to craftsmanship.

---

## The Dogra Period (1846–1947)

International demand for Kashmiri shawls expanded dramatically.

European collectors developed a deep appreciation for Kashmiri weaving.

Historic shawls entered royal collections, museums, and private estates.

The distinctive boteh motif inspired the famous Paisley pattern, influencing textile design around the world.

---

## Late 19th Century

Industrial textile production transformed global markets.

Machine-made imitations became increasingly common.

Authentic handwoven pashmina faced growing competition from mass-produced alternatives.

Yet true craftsmanship retained its prestige among discerning collectors.

---

## Early 20th Century

Traditional workshops continued to operate despite changing economic conditions.

Artisan knowledge remained largely within families.

Many techniques were still transmitted through apprenticeship rather than written instruction.

---

## Mid-20th Century

Following political and economic changes across South Asia, Kashmiri handicrafts entered a new phase.

Government institutions, cooperatives, and cultural organisations began supporting the preservation of traditional crafts.

International recognition of Kashmiri textiles continued to grow.

---

## Late 20th Century

Scientific research improved understanding of pashmina fibre.

Museums expanded textile conservation programmes.

Universities increased research into Himalayan pastoralism, textile history, and artisan communities.

Collectors developed greater interest in provenance and authenticity.

---

## 21st Century

The internet transformed global access to Kashmiri craftsmanship.

Customers could now learn directly from artisans and heritage brands.

At the same time, misuse of the word “pashmina” became widespread, making consumer education increasingly important.

Efforts to protect authentic Kashmiri pashmina through Geographical Indication (GI) registration, certification, and awareness became central to preserving the integrity of the craft.

---

## Looking Forward

The next chapter in the history of pashmina has not yet been written.

It will depend on:

* The resilience of artisan communities.
* The protection of traditional knowledge.
* Responsible stewardship of natural resources.
* Continued appreciation for handcrafted excellence.
* Honest communication between makers and buyers.

Every generation contributes another thread to this continuing story.
