The word Pashmina originates from the Persian word pashm, referring to exceptionally fine underfleece. Unlike ordinary sheep’s wool, pashmina is obtained from the soft undercoat that grows beneath the coarse outer hair of mountain goats adapted to some of the harshest climates on Earth.

Every winter, these goats develop an insulating layer of extraordinarily fine fibres that protects them against freezing temperatures and powerful Himalayan winds. When spring arrives, this undercoat is naturally shed. Traditionally, it is collected by carefully combing the animal rather than shearing it, preserving the delicate fibres for further processing.

The finest of these fibres have, for centuries, formed the foundation of Kashmir’s most celebrated textile tradition.

Although the fibre originates in the high-altitude regions of Ladakh and neighbouring Himalayan plateaus, it is in Kashmir that the material undergoes its remarkable transformation. Through processes of careful cleaning, hand spinning, weaving, finishing, and, in many cases, embroidery, the raw fibre becomes a textile recognised around the world for its softness, warmth, lightness, and artistic refinement.

A genuine Kashmiri pashmina shawl is therefore not defined solely by its fibre. It represents the union of nature, geography, craftsmanship, and cultural heritage.

Over generations, specialised artisans developed techniques for every stage of production. Women traditionally carried out delicate processes such as manual dehairing and hand spinning, while skilled weavers transformed the yarn into fabric on wooden looms. Embroiderers added intricate Sozni needlework, and master weavers created Kani shawls using coded design systems known as Taleem.

The resulting textile became more than an article of clothing. It became a symbol of refinement, diplomacy, craftsmanship, and cultural identity.

Today, authentic Kashmiri pashmina continues to represent one of the finest achievements of traditional textile production. Yet it also faces modern challenges, including imitation products, incorrect labelling, and the gradual disappearance of traditional skills. Understanding the complete story of pashmina is therefore essential not only for appreciating its beauty but also for helping preserve a living heritage.
