Traditional Pashmina Processing in Kashmir

An authentic Kashmiri pashmina shawl is not created in a single workshop, nor by a single artisan. It is the result of a carefully coordinated sequence of traditional skills, many of which have remained remarkably consistent for centuries. Each stage prepares the fibre for the next, and together they transform a delicate natural undercoat into one of the world’s finest textiles.

Unlike industrial textile production, traditional pashmina making depends on patience rather than speed. Every process demands judgement, experience, and an understanding of the behaviour of an exceptionally fine fibre.

---

## Step 1 — Harvesting the Fibre

The journey begins in the high-altitude regions where the Changthangi goat naturally sheds its winter undercoat during spring.

Rather than shearing the animal, herders traditionally collect the fibre by gently combing the goat. This method separates much of the valuable underfleece while leaving the coarser outer hairs largely intact.

The collected material is still far from ready for weaving. It contains guard hairs, dust, vegetable matter, and other natural impurities that must be removed through a series of careful manual processes.

---

## Step 2 — Puch-Nawun: Manual Dehairing

One of the most specialised stages in traditional processing is Puch-Nawun, the manual separation of the soft underfibre from the coarse guard hairs.

Historically, this work has been carried out primarily by skilled women. Using their hands and trained eyes, they separate the valuable pashmina fibres strand by strand.

Although modern mechanical dehairing machines now exist, many artisans continue to value careful manual dehairing for delicate fibres because it allows greater control over the selection of usable material.

At the end of this stage, the fibre has become significantly cleaner but still requires further preparation before spinning.

---

## Step 3 — Combing

The cleaned fibre is repeatedly combed to remove remaining dust, vegetable fragments, and short fibres while aligning the longer fibres in a parallel direction.

Traditional combs consist of closely spaced metal teeth fixed to wooden supports. The artisan repeatedly draws small tufts of fibre across the comb until the material becomes soft, clean, and evenly aligned.

This stage has two important purposes:

* It removes remaining impurities.
* It prepares the fibres for smooth hand spinning.

The quality of spinning depends greatly upon the care taken during combing.

---

## Step 4 — Kharioat: Rice Starch Preparation

One of the most distinctive features of traditional Kashmiri processing is the temporary application of powdered rice.

Known locally as Kharioat, finely pounded rice is sprinkled over the prepared fibre and allowed to rest before being combed again.

This traditional treatment provides gentle cohesion to the delicate fibres, making them easier to spin while helping preserve softness and flexibility.

After the excess powder has been removed, the fibre is shaped into small bundles known locally as Thumb.

Each thumb contains just enough prepared fibre for the spinner to work comfortably by hand.

---

## Step 5 — Hand Spinning on the Yander

The prepared fibre now reaches one of the most iconic stages of the entire craft.

Using a traditional spinning wheel called the Yander (also known as a Charkha), skilled spinners gradually draw fibres from the thumb while simultaneously twisting them into an extremely fine continuous yarn.

This process demands extraordinary coordination.

The spinner controls fibre thickness using the fingers of one hand while rotating the wheel with the other. The slightest inconsistency may produce uneven yarn or cause the delicate fibres to break.

Master spinners spend years developing the touch required for this work.

The finished yarn is wound onto small holders before later being twisted and prepared for weaving.

---

## Step 6 — Preparing the Warp

Before weaving can begin, the spun yarn must undergo several additional preparations.

The yarn is washed, dried, and, where required, dyed. It is then treated with a light starch solution known as Maya, which provides sufficient strength for the warp threads without permanently altering the character of the fibre.

The yarn is wound onto wooden spools known as Prech before undergoing the careful arrangement required for weaving.

Traditionally, around twelve hundred warp threads may be prepared together, enough for several shawls depending on their dimensions.

---

## Step 7 — Setting the Loom

Preparing the loom is itself a specialised occupation.

Every individual warp thread must pass through heddles and reeds in the correct sequence before weaving can begin.

This painstaking preparation may take several days, reflecting the precision required before the first row of fabric is even woven.

Only after the loom has been correctly dressed can the weaver begin transforming yarn into cloth.

---

## A Chain of Knowledge

By this stage, a remarkable number of skilled hands have already contributed to the future shawl:

* The pastoralist who cared for the goat.
* The collector who harvested the fibre.
* The artisan who removed the guard hairs.
* The woman who combed the fibre.
* The spinner who created the yarn.
* The specialist who prepared the warp.

The weaver has not yet woven a single thread, yet the shawl already embodies the knowledge of an entire community.
